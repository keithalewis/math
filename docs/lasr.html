I assume you know about vector space scalar multiplication, vector addition, and the laws they satisfy. The scalars must be a field such as the real numbers \boldsymbol{R} or complex numbers \boldsymbol{C}. Another example is \boldsymbol{Z}_p = \boldsymbol{Z}/p\boldsymbol{Z} of integers modulo a prime p. We will use \boldsymbol{F} to denote a generic field of scalars.

Linear Operator

A linear operator is a function T\colon V\to W between two vector spaces that preserves the vector space structure: T(ax + y) = a(Tx) + Ty for scalar a\in\boldsymbol{F} and vectors x,y\in V. Note the scalar multiplication and vector addition on the left-hand side are those for V and on the right-hand side are those for W.

We write \boldsymbol{0}_V for the zero of vector addition in V: \boldsymbol{0}_V + v = v = v + \boldsymbol{0}_V.

Exercise: Show v + v = v implies v = \boldsymbol{0}_V.

Hint: Add -v to both sides.

Solution If v + v = v then \begin{aligned} (v + v) - v &= v - v \\ v + (v - v) &= v - v \\ v + \boldsymbol{0}&= \boldsymbol{0}\\ v &= \boldsymbol{0}\\ \end{aligned}

Exercise: Show for any linear operator T\colon V\to W that T\boldsymbol{0}_V = \boldsymbol{0}_W.

Hint: T(\boldsymbol{0}+ \boldsymbol{0}) = T\boldsymbol{0}.

Exercise: Show T(x + y) = Tx + Ty.

Hint: Use 1x = x.

Exercise: Show T(ax) = a(Tx).

Hint: Use T(\boldsymbol{0}_V) = \boldsymbol{0}_W.

Subspace

A subspace of a vector space V is a subset U\subseteq V that is closed under scalar multiplication and vector addition. We can write this as {\boldsymbol{F}U \subseteq U} and {U + U\subseteq U} where {\boldsymbol{F}U = \{au\mid a\in\boldsymbol{F}, u\in U\}} and {U + U = \{u + u'\mid u,u'\in U\}}.

Exercise: If U and U' are subspaces show U\cap U' and U + U' are subspaces.

Hint. Show they are closed under scalar multiplication and vector addition.

Structure

The kernel of a linear operator T\colon V\to W is {\ker T = \{v\in V\mid Tv = \boldsymbol{0}\}\subseteq V} and the range is {\operatorname{ran}T = \{Tv\mid v\in V\}\subseteq W}.

Exercise Show \ker T is a subspace of V and \operatorname{ran}T is a subspace of W.

The first fundamental fact about the structure of a linear operator {T\colon V\to W} is there are linear operators {V \to V/\ker T \to \operatorname{ran}T \to W}. The first linear operator is onto/surjective, the second linear operator is one-to-one and onto/bijective and the third linear operater is one-to-one/injective.

The second operator demystifies the various pseudo inverse notions people still stuck in the Gilbert Strang row and column vector tediousness suffer through. Every linear operator from {V/\ker T\to\operatorname{ran}T} is invertible.

Exercise Show V\to V/\ker V where v\mapsto v + \ker T is onto.

Hint. It is well-defined if v + \ker T = v' + \ker T implies Tv = Tv'. It is clearly onto. Show if Tv = Tv' then v + \ker T = v' + \ker T using the previous exercises.

If S\colon U\to V and T\colon V\to W are linear operators we say they are exact if \operatorname{ran}S = \ker T, S is injective, and T is surjective.
